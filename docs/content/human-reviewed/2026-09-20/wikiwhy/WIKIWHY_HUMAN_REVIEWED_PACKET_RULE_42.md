## Passage 1: What Can an Animal-Vision Test Prove?

**Runtime ID:** wikiwhy-01

**Source and rights:** C. Lloyd Morgan, *Animal Life and Intelligence*,
[Project Gutenberg ebook 49818](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/49818), and
Robert Mearns Yerkes, *The Dancing Mouse*,
[Project Gutenberg ebook 8729](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8729). Both
works are public domain in the USA. The Yerkes excerpt uses two approved clarity edits: “dancer” becomes “mouse” and “dancers” becomes “mice”; other source wording is unchanged. The source introductions and transition are project-written.

### Exact complete spoken passage

> Two excerpts examine what animal-vision tests can prove.
>
> First, an excerpt from *Animal Life and Intelligence* by C. Lloyd Morgan.
>
> Sir John Lubbock was unable to teach his intelligent dog Van to distinguish
> between coloured cards; but the failure was as complete when the cards were
> marked respectively with one, two, or three dark bands. We are not justified,
> therefore, in ascribing the failure to colour-blindness. The real failure,
> probably, was in each case to make the animal understand what was wanted.
>
> Next, an excerpt from *The Dancing Mouse* by Robert Mearns Yerkes.
>
> Too frequently investigators, in their work on vision in animals, have
> assumed that brightness vision and color vision are inseparable; or, if not
> making this assumption, they have failed to realize that the same wave-length
> probably has markedly different effects upon the retinal elements of the eyes
> of unlike organisms. In a study of the sense of sight it is extremely
> important to discover whether difference in the quality, as well as in the
> intensity, of a visual stimulus influences the organism; in other words,
> whether color sensitiveness, as well as brightness sensitiveness, is present.
> If the mouse perceives only brightness or luminosity, and not color, it is
> evident that its visual world is strikingly different from that of the normal
> human being. The experiments now to be described were planned to show what the
> facts really are.
>
> After describing tests with violet and red cards, Yerkes reports:
>
> Although we are not justified in concluding from this experiment that mice
> cannot be taught to distinguish violet from red, there certainly is good
> ground for the statement that they do not readily discriminate between these
> colors.

### Vocabulary

1. **ascribing**
   - **Definition:** Explaining something by assigning it a particular cause.
   - **Exact playback phrase:** “In this passage, Morgan avoids ascribing the
     dog's failure to color-blindness.”

2. **luminosity**
   - **Definition:** The brightness of something that gives off or reflects
     light.
   - **Exact playback phrase:** “In this passage, luminosity means the
     brightness an animal perceives.”

3. **discriminate**
   - **Definition:** To notice a difference and tell two things apart.
   - **Exact playback phrase:** “In this passage, the mice do not readily
     discriminate between violet and red.”

### Quick check

**Exact question:** What problem do both excerpts identify in animal-vision
tests?

- **A — Correct:** A failed test may reflect misunderstanding or brightness
  cues rather than an inability to perceive color.
- **B:** A failed test mainly shows that the same wavelength affects the eyes of
  different species differently.
- **C:** Failing both a color task and a dark-band task is reliable evidence of
  color-blindness.

## Passage 9: An Essay Concerning Human Understanding

### Exact complete spoken passage

> An excerpt from “An Essay Concerning Human Understanding” by John Locke.
>
> If I myself see a man walk on the ice, it is past probability; it is
> knowledge. But if another tells me he saw a man in England, in the midst of a
> sharp winter, walk upon water hardened with cold, this has so great conformity
> with what is usually observed to happen, that I am disposed by the nature of
> the thing itself to assent to it; unless some manifest suspicion attend the
> relation of that matter of fact.
>
> But if the same thing be told to one born between the tropics, who never saw
> nor heard of any such thing before, there the whole probability relies on
> testimony. As the relators are more in number, and of more credit, and have no
> interest to speak contrary to the truth, so that matter of fact is like to
> find more or less belief.
>
> Though to a man whose experience has always been quite contrary, and who has
> never heard of anything like it, the most untainted credit of a witness will
> scarce be able to find belief. As it happened to a Dutch ambassador, who
> entertained the king of Siam with the particularities of Holland, which he
> was inquisitive after. Amongst other things he told him that the water in his
> country would sometimes, in cold weather, be so hard that men walked upon it,
> and that it would bear an elephant, if he were there. To which the king
> replied, “Hitherto I have believed the strange things you have told me,
