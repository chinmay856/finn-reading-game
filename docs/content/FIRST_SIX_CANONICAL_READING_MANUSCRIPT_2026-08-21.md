**Spoken passage**

This is What Dogs Can See, an original synthesis based on Color Vision in the Dog, by Jay Neitz, Timothy Geist, and Gerald Jacobs, and Are Dogs Red-Green Colour Blind?, by Marcello Siniscalchi and colleagues.

A dog's world is not colorless. Experiments have found that dogs have dichromatic vision, meaning that their color system relies on two main kinds of cone cells rather than the three used by most humans. The two cone pigments are most sensitive to short and medium-to-long wavelengths of light. In everyday terms, blues and yellows can remain distinct, while reds and greens may be harder to separate.

Researchers have tested this in more than one way. In one early study, dogs learned to choose among colored lights. Their choices fit a two-cone model. A later behavioral study adapted a color test often used with people. Dogs again responded in a pattern resembling human red-green color blindness. These methods do not let a scientist experience a dog's private view. They do let scientists make careful predictions from repeatable behavior and measurements.

Brightness complicates the question. An animal might choose a card because it looks lighter, not because its color looks different. Another experiment therefore asked whether untrained dogs would rely more on color or brightness when both clues were available. In that test, color was often the more useful clue. The result challenged an older assumption that color played little part in ordinary canine behavior.

The supported conclusion is narrower than “dogs see only blue and yellow.” A surface reflects many wavelengths, lighting changes their appearance, and vision includes brightness, motion, contrast, and context. Evidence supports a two-cone color system and predictable difficulty with some color differences. It does not produce a perfect photograph of what every dog sees.

This is Wikipedia: Revision, Not Revelation, an original synthesis based on the Wikipedia and History of Wikipedia articles by Wikipedia contributors under CC BY-SA 4.0.

Wikipedia can look like a finished encyclopedia, but every article is also the current result of an editing process. A reader sees the latest version first. Behind it sits a history page recording earlier versions, timestamps, edit summaries, and the accounts or network addresses that submitted changes. The history does not guarantee that every edit was wise. It makes change inspectable.

The project began in 2001 with an unusual wager: volunteers could build a reference work in public, and imperfect pages could improve through continued revision. That openness created obvious risks. A person can introduce an error, remove useful context, or make a claim sound more certain than its source. Openness also gives other editors tools to compare versions, restore material, discuss disagreements, and ask for better citations.

Wikipedia's policies distinguish the article from the sources beneath it. Editors are expected to summarize published sources rather than treat their own beliefs as evidence. Important claims should be verifiable. Disputed topics may need careful wording that represents significant viewpoints in proportion to reliable coverage. These rules are goals enforced by people, not magic properties of the software.

For a reader, the useful question is not simply, “Is Wikipedia true?” A stronger set of questions is: What claim does this article make? Which source supports that exact claim? When was the page last changed? Does the cited source say what the article says it does? An article can be a helpful beginning while its links, history, and uncertainty remain visible.

### 10. Calibrated Confidence

**Canonical ID:** `wikiwhy-10`

**Spoken passage**

This is Calibrated Confidence, by the Finn Reading Game editorial team. It is editorial essay created for the Finn Reading Game.

Confidence is useful when it follows the evidence. A weather forecaster, mechanic, or doctor must often act without perfect knowledge. Saying “nothing can be known” would be as unhelpful as pretending every guess is certain. The better skill is calibration: matching the strength of a statement to the strength of its support.

Suppose three careful measurements agree. That agreement may justify more confidence than one hurried observation. It does not prove that every future measurement must match. A conclusion also depends on what was measured, how it was measured, and whether another explanation fits the result. Evidence can narrow the possibilities without reducing them to one.

Certain words help a writer show this structure. “Demonstrates” signals a stronger claim than “suggests.” “Usually” leaves room for exceptions that “always” would erase. “In this sample” tells the reader where a result was observed. These phrases are not decorations added by a timid writer. They carry information about limits.

Uncertainty can come from several places. A sample may be small. An instrument may be imprecise. Two causes may be difficult to separate. Researchers may understand a pattern without knowing the mechanism behind it. Naming the uncertainty helps another person decide what to test next.

A calibrated conclusion therefore has two parts. It says what the available evidence supports, and it says what remains unresolved. That form may sound less dramatic than a giant certainty badge.
