# Ten-site plot contracts — candidate for Chinmay review

Status: **historical research/plot draft; interactive-investigation structure
superseded by `TEN_SITE_READING_MISSIONS_2026-08-14.md`**

These plots turn Chinmay's lesson directions into ten distinct, teen-legible
stories. Real platforms inspire the transferable skills, but the exaggerated
malfunctions belong to the fictional game. A source supports the premise; it
does not imply that the real service literally performs the fictional act.

Platform help pages establish only the feature or policy they describe. They do
not establish how often a harm occurs, whether safeguards are effective, or that
the fictional malfunction occurs on the real service. Platform self-descriptions
must not stand alone for prevalence, impact, or fairness claims.

## Shared plot grammar

Every site follows a seven-beat rhythm without repeating identical dialogue:

1. **Useful promise** — show why a reasonable person likes this kind of site.
2. **Funny contradiction** — the corruption is visible before anyone explains it.
3. **Reading clue** — highlighted reading reveals evidence needed in the site.
4. **Player hypothesis** — the player interprets the clue before Amy confirms it.
5. **Bounded choice** — two or three plausible actions; no glowing “smart” answer.
6. **Midpoint reversal** — the system changes tactics without erasing prior work.
7. **Secured consequence** — the player applies one reusable question and sees a
   lasting change.

Amy helps interpret evidence, Chinmay creates comic pressure, and Techno may
expose a physical contradiction. None of them should solve the site for Finn.

Every site also shares a nonpunitive safety contract:

- persistent `Pause`, `Back`, and `Try again` controls;
- wrong choices reveal more evidence rather than erase progress or ridicule Finn;
- the midpoint explicitly preserves reading results and collected evidence;
- `Unknown`, `Not enough evidence`, `Do nothing yet`, or `Ask for help` can be
  successful outcomes when the story supports them;
- fictional messages, carts, accounts, and routes are clearly practice scenarios
  with no real-world action;
- sensitive situations end with a short recap of what is safe and what reusable
  action the player now controls.

## Lesson separation matrix

| Site | One transferable question | Deliberately not about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WikiWhy | Does the cited evidence actually support this claim? | Search ranking or popularity |
| ThreadIt | Are these independent confirmations or copies of one claim? | Whether a community is good or bad |
| FacePlace | Am I inferring someone's whole life or feelings from one curated moment? | Impersonation, news coverage, or generic screen-time fear |
| MyCorner | Can I verify this person's identity another way? | Aesthetic self-expression or profile popularity |
| Yahuh | What important news is missing from this personalized menu? | Claim-level citation checking |
| ViewTube | Who is choosing what I watch next? | Whether every recommended video is false |
| Search-ish | Which result actually fits my question, and what facts can I check about it? | Whether encyclopedias are trustworthy or whether repetition equals consensus |
| Amaze-On | What criteria—not placement—should control this purchase? | Whether cheap products or ads are always bad |
| Spotty-Fi | Who made this track, who consented, and who receives credit? | Whether all AI-assisted art is illegitimate |
| MapGuess | What destination and real-world anchors support this route? | Whether navigation tools are generally unreliable |

## 1. WikiWhy — The Article That Hated “Maybe”

**Lesson:** An encyclopedia is a map to evidence, not the evidence itself.

**Useful promise:** Finn opens WikiWhy because it is a fast way to understand an
unfamiliar topic and find sources for a class poster he is building with a friend:
`Do dogs see only black and white?`

**Corrupted contradiction:** The article looks impressively polished, but every
qualifier—`may`, `often`, `according to`—vanishes. A giant confidence badge rises
to `100%` while citation connections quietly snap.

**Reading clue:** The passage includes the wording of two underlying sources. One
says dogs perceive a narrower range of colors than humans; the other does not
support the black-and-white claim at all. The player highlights the mismatch
before confidently misinforming the class—and Techno—on the finished poster.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** “Which sentence can these sources actually
support?” The options are three plausible phrasings with different levels of
certainty, not true/false buttons.

**Midpoint reversal:** After the player repairs several sentences, the system
rewrites the visible article to sound certain again. Saved reading results and
source cards stay visibly locked; only the article layer is attacked.

**Secured consequence:** Finn answers one reusable question—`What wording does
the source actually support?`—and physically connects the honest claim to its
evidence. One disputed claim correctly ends as `CONTESTED — KEEP CONTEXT` rather
than being forced into certainty. The confidence badge disappears; evidence
connections remain visible and resist the rewrite.

**Character/comedy seed:** Chinmay: “It had way too many maybes. Nobody clicks
those.” Techno pins the one source card whose text does not match the claim.

**Do not claim:** Wiki-style encyclopedias are inherently false, open editing is
inherently bad, published or primary sources are automatically reliable, or
citation count automatically proves truth.

**Evidence base:** Wikipedia's own guidance treats verifiability and reliable
published sourcing as central and recommends using the encyclopedia as a
starting point whose citations and history can be inspected:
[Verifiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability),
[Researching with Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Researching_with_Wikipedia).

**Visual translation seed:** Familiar article column, contents rail, transformed
globe cue, neutral-blue grammar, visible citation cables, and original typography,
icons, spacing, and copy.

## 2. ThreadIt — Ten Accounts, One Rumor

**Lesson:** Repetition and votes do not create independent confirmation.

**Useful promise:** Finn opens a community thread because people there share
fast local updates, explanations, jokes, and firsthand experience. He is checking
whether a school game has been cancelled before telling his friends not to come.

**Corrupted contradiction:** A dramatic rumor has thousands of votes. Replies
appear before the buried original question, and the same oddly specific phrase
keeps appearing under different avatars.

**Reading clue:** The passage provides three details that distinguish a repost
from an independent report: repeated wording, impossible timing, and the same
cropped image.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** The player traces three readable branches:
one exact repost, one changed caption using the same image, and one low-vote
firsthand update with genuinely new evidence. Finn can also conclude `Not enough
new information yet` before sharing anything.

**Midpoint reversal:** After Finn marks two copies, the system merges all their
votes into a larger `CONFIRMED BY THE COMMUNITY` badge and hides the low-vote
firsthand update. Finn must preserve the origin trace and decide whether the new
evidence is sufficient before sharing.

**Secured consequence:** Finn pauses sharing and restores three plain-language
lanes: `First post`, `Copies`, and `New information`. He checks the relevant
official event notice before deciding whether to share. The final thread preserves
useful disagreement rather than manufacturing a tidy consensus.

**Character/comedy seed:** Amy: “One post wearing ten hats.” Chinmay: “I
pre-agreed with myself.” Techno's ball rolls down several branches and returns to
the same root.

**Do not claim:** Crowds are always wrong, anonymous people are inherently
dishonest, identical wording always proves manipulation, or votes have no useful
community function.

**Evidence base:** Reddit explains that votes affect what is surfaced and that
personalized feeds use prior activity; its own rules discourage vote manipulation
and hoaxes: [Votes](https://support.reddithelp.com/hc/en-us/articles/7419626610708-What-are-upvotes-and-downvotes),
[Feed recommendations](https://support.reddithelp.com/hc/en-us/articles/4402284777364-What-are-home-feed-recommendations),
[Reddiquette](https://support.reddithelp.com/hc/en-us/articles/205926439-Reddiquette).

**Visual translation seed:** Nested community grammar reduced to three readable
branches, an original vote icon family, a transformed warm accent, and a source
trace that can be understood without reading every comment.

## 3. FacePlace — The Perfect Day, Cropped

**Lesson:** A curated happy post can be real without revealing someone's whole
day or feelings. Respond without turning one selected moment into an assumption.

**Useful promise:** Finn uses FacePlace to keep up with friends and share moments
that matter to them. A flood of perfect party photos makes him briefly assume he
was the only person who had an ordinary, messy day.

**Corrupted contradiction:** Every card shows the same impossibly perfect
celebration. The feed declares `EVERYONE IS THRIVING` while an absurd reaction
meter climbs from `12%` to `114%` to `AVOCADO%`.

**Reading clue:** A short account of the event mentions the ordinary moments
outside the photo: rain, a missed bus, a friend helping clean up, and Techno just
outside the frame. The player notices that the happy post is genuine but the feed
repeatedly excludes ordinary context. Finn never diagnoses the poster's feelings
or demands private disclosure.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** Finn compares two feeds, asks `What got left
out?`, and chooses among three believable responses: react as if the perfect post
is the whole story, ask a neutral question about the day, or wait for more context.
The reading supports caution without dictating that a friend owes Finn private
information.

**Midpoint reversal:** After Finn expands the frame, the system recrops the same
photo into an even more impressive version and boosts `AVOCADO%`. Finn's collected
context remains saved outside the feed card, proving that the system changed the
selection rather than the underlying day.

**Secured consequence:** The final composition preserves `POSTED MOMENT` beside
`WHAT ELSE HAPPENED`; neither invalidates the other. The reusable skill is
noticing what a selected feed does not show, not assuming a hidden private story
or trying to diagnose a friend's emotions.

**Character/comedy seed:** Chinmay worries, “No score? How will people know
whether they're impressed?” Techno sits outside the photo crop until it expands.

**Do not claim:** Happy posts are lies, people should not share good moments, or
social media has one inevitable mental-health effect for every teen.

**Evidence base:** The APA recommends helping adolescents examine accuracy,
representativeness, manipulated imagery, and social comparison while recognizing
that effects vary by person and context:
[APA health advisory](https://www.apa.org/topics/social-media-internet/health-advisory-adolescent-social-media-use).
The [U.S. Surgeon General's advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/surgeongeneral/reports-and-publications/youth-mental-health/social-media/index.html)
also describes both benefits and risks.

**Visual translation seed:** Social-feed grammar with an original blue family,
uncropping cards, two visibly different feed windows, and no copied logo, icons,
or real-user imagery.

## 4. MyCorner — The Profile That Knows Too Much

**Lesson:** A convincing profile is not proof of identity; verify through another
channel.

**Useful promise:** MyCorner is a playful space for profiles that express music,
friends, interests, and personal style.

**Corrupted contradiction:** A polished duplicate Amy profile appears with copied
photos, favorite music, and old posts. It urgently asks Finn to send an unreleased
group-project video. A persistent notice says `PRACTICE MESSAGE — NOTHING SENT`.
One timeline says the account was created today while displaying “memories” from
years earlier.

**Reading clue:** The passage describes three meaningful warning signs: urgency,
an impossible account timeline, and a request that Amy would normally make
through a different channel.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** Finn uses a compatible safety sequence rather
than a trick question: `Pause`, `Contact Amy another way`, then `Block / report`.
“Another way” means a previously known contact or in-person check, never contact
information supplied by the suspicious profile. `Ask someone you trust` remains
available.

The exercise never asks Finn to click a link, download a file, send personal
information or media, contact the suspicious profile, or investigate a real
person. For money, threats, private information, or sexual content, `Tell a
trusted adult` is a prominent safe outcome rather than a loss condition.

**Midpoint reversal:** The duplicate becomes more visually convincing by copying
the real profile's theme. Instead of playing “spot the weird avatar,” the player
checks history and outside confirmation. The photos peel back to show their
original source.

**Secured consequence:** The duplicate is isolated and labeled `IDENTITY NOT
CONFIRMED`; copied material is removed and Amy's real profile remains intact.
The game confirms `NO MESSAGE SENT / NO ACCOUNT ACCESSED`. Finn keeps a reusable
`Verify another way` action for future urgent messages. Convincing impersonation
is the deceiver's responsibility, not the target's failure.

**Character/comedy seed:** The corrupted system briefly tries to duplicate
Techno's profile too, but every copied field still reads `INTERESTS: BALL`.

**Do not claim:** New accounts, pseudonyms, avatars, visual differences, or lack
of a badge prove deception. Verification behavior—not profiling—is the skill.

**Evidence base:** The FTC warns that scammers can copy or compromise profiles
and recommends checking unexpected requests through a known route:
[FTC impersonation guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/10/friend-request-could-be-scammer).

**Visual translation seed:** Expressive personal-profile grammar, two nearly
matching but independently drawn pages, source-peel layers, and owner-controlled
themes that never reuse real legacy assets.

## 5. Yahuh Portal — The News Menu With Half the Day Missing

**Lesson:** A personalized news feed is a menu chosen for you, not a complete map
of what matters.

**Useful promise:** Yahuh gives Finn a fast front page for news, weather, sports,
and topics he follows. Finn and Amy need the same bus to reach a school event on
time, and each assumes the other's personalized page will show any important change.

**Corrupted contradiction:** Finn and Amy open the portal at the same time but see
two incompatible versions of the day. Finn gets sports and entertainment; Amy
gets technology and weather. Each page insists `YOU HAVE SEEN EVERYTHING`.

**Reading clue:** The passage identifies a concrete local bus-route change that
matters to both of them but appears on neither personalized page.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** Finn chooses whether to trust his page, trust
Amy's page, or temporarily widen beyond both personalized feeds. The reading clue
shows why widening matters without naming the correct button.

**Midpoint reversal:** The portal's hidden `LIKELY TO CLICK` dial is revealed. As
the system turns it up, the two feeds narrow further and all cards begin changing
at exactly the same timestamp.

**Secured consequence:** Finn adds a visible `Local / top stories` lane beside
`For you` and can compare reporting origins. `News`, `Opinion`, and `Sponsored`
labels appear as unscored supporting context rather than three more lessons.
Personalization remains useful but visibly partial; the page promises a `broader
view`, never a complete view.

**Character/comedy seed:** A weather alert mutates into “stormy markets,” a
Thunder sports result, and umbrella shopping. Techno's ball is correctly labeled
`DOG TOY — NOT BREAKING NEWS`.

**Do not claim:** Yahuh fabricates reporting, personalization always traps every
user, all news ranking is selected only for ad revenue, or there is one politically
neutral or objectively perfect front page.

**Evidence base:** Yahoo documents interest-based recommendations and controls:
[Homepage experience](https://help.yahoo.com/kb/homepage/overview-yahoo-homepage-experience-sln36655.html),
[News interests and blocked publishers](https://help.yahoo.com/kb/SLN36665.html).
MediaSmarts teaches that a curated feed can provide an incomplete picture:
[Networked News](https://mediasmarts.ca/teacher-resources/networked-news-understanding-and-curating-your-digital-news-feed).

**Visual translation seed:** Dense portal grammar with a transformed purple
family, two side-by-side personalized states, a visible attention dial, and
original headlines, imagery, icons, and module proportions.

## 6. ViewTube — Who Picked “Up Next”?

**Lesson:** `Up next` predicts what Finn may want to watch; it is not a truth,
importance, or quality score. The player decides whether the chain continues.

**Useful promise:** Finn opens ViewTube for entertaining explainers, creators,
and fast updates from people he follows.

**Corrupted contradiction:** Finn states a simple viewing intention—`Understand
the school notice`—but a partial clip titled `SCHOOL CLOSED TOMORROW!` starts an
autoplay tunnel of increasingly dramatic reactions. A silent countdown keeps
selecting videos that drift farther from Finn's goal. Pause remains available
from the start.

**Reading clue:** The passage reminds Finn of his original goal—understand the
notice before meeting friends in ten minutes—and shows how much time each possible
next video would consume. The cropped school claim remains comic background, not
another full source-evaluation puzzle.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** Finn pauses the chain and chooses among three
individually reasonable videos serving different goals: complete context, quick
entertainment, or more reactions. He constructs a short queue that answers his
stated question within the available time and deliberately chooses when to stop.

**Midpoint reversal:** The fictional `PERFECT FEED` overwrites Finn's careful
queue with louder variants, claiming they are more likely to interest him. Finn
reasserts the original viewing goal; prior reading and queue choices remain saved.

**Secured consequence:** Finn restores a short intentional queue that answers his
question, includes one video chosen simply for fun, and ends on purpose. The
status changes from `SELECTED FOR YOU` to `SELECTED BY YOU`; autoplay remains an
explicit optional control. Source accuracy remains background flavor, not a
fourth source-tracing lesson.

**Character/comedy seed:** Techno paws the silent autoplay toggle off only after
Finn identifies it. Chinmay: “The next video was getting impatient.”

**Do not claim:** Recommendation systems deliberately radicalize everyone, watch
time is their only input, or every recommended video is false or harmful.

**Evidence base:** YouTube describes signals used for recommendations and the
different role of the current video in `Up next`:
[Recommendation basics](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16089387?hl=en).
It documents autoplay as a controllable feature and says it is off by default for
ages 13–17: [Autoplay](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6327615?hl=en-GB).

**Visual translation seed:** Player-plus-thumbnail-rail grammar, an original red
family and control system, a branching autoplay tunnel, and no copied play mark,
thumbnails, creator data, or controls.

## 7. Search-ish — The Best-Looking Wrong Source

**Lesson:** A search result is a lead, not an answer. The useful source is the one
that fits Finn's exact question and exposes checkable facts about itself.

**Useful promise:** Search-ish quickly finds useful starting points across many
different kinds of information.

**Corrupted contradiction:** A confident instant answer appears for Finn's model-
city project: `Which glue works on foam board without melting it?` The top result
is a store selling “universal” glue, another is an outdated craft post, a third is
a plain manufacturer compatibility sheet, and a polished summary has no author.
Ranking and visual polish keep swapping places with relevance while the model's
test corner begins to curl.

**Reading clue:** The passage states the material, drying-time need, and test
condition. Result cards expose checkable author, date, and purpose facts. Those
facts all serve one judgment—does this source answer this exact question?—rather
than becoming separate lessons. No magical trust score chooses for Finn.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** The player opens `Where did this come from?` and
chooses which result best fits the specific question, then identifies what that
result still cannot establish. `Not enough evidence yet` is a valid conclusion.
Polish, domain suffix, and ranking position are not sufficient clues.

**Midpoint reversal:** The instant answer begins stitching together incompatible
pieces from the store, old article, and dataset. It remains on screen but gains
`DOES THIS ANSWER YOUR QUESTION?` while the result purposes become visible.

**Secured consequence:** Finn selects one fit-for-purpose source, performs the
small supplied test, and records one remaining uncertainty rather than pretending
the answer is universal. Sponsored results remain clearly separated rather than
treated as automatically false. Search-ish teaches source fitness; ThreadIt alone
owns the many-copies/one-origin mechanic.

**Character/comedy seed:** Chinmay: “The first result was closest to the top.” Amy
waits for Finn to explain why that does not answer the project's question.

**Do not claim:** Organic ranking is sold to advertisers, the top result is always
wrong, primary or institutional sources are automatically neutral, or a familiar
logo, `.org`, `.edu`, or polished design proves credibility.

**Evidence base:** Google describes ranking as an automated relevance process and
separates organic results from ads:
[Ranking systems](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ranking-systems-guide),
[Search results and ads](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722080?hl=en).
Its `About this result` guidance recommends examining source background and what
other sites say: [Learn about a page](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/11127743?hl=en).

**Visual translation seed:** Central search field, results stack, transformed
multicolored-letter rhythm with a different typeface, silhouette, color order,
icons, spacing, and result-card treatment. Result cards visibly reveal `Who`,
`When`, and `Purpose` rather than collapsing into a source tree.

## 8. Amaze-On — The Cheapest Cart Costs More

**Lesson:** Placement may reflect advertising or sales goals, so first, cheapest,
or sponsored is not automatically best for Finn. Set needs, compare, and confirm.

**Useful promise:** Amaze-On makes it convenient to compare products. Finn needs a
phone tripod under $25 that can stand at desk height for a video project due Friday.

**Corrupted contradiction:** A persistent banner reads `PRACTICE CART — NO REAL
ORDER OR PAYMENT`. Inside it, `HELPFUL SHOPPER` keeps ranking a tiny sponsored
table stand first because its sticker price is lowest, even though it cannot meet
the height requirement. Boxes and replacement packaging visualize the cost of a
poor fit without implying that cheap or sponsored products are inherently bad.

**Reading clue:** The passage describes the project's actual needs, budget, and
three checkable criteria: `Works for this`, `Lasts`, and `Total cost`.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** Finn can freeze the cart and compare credible
options, including buying new, borrowing/used where appropriate, or buying
nothing yet. Cheap and sponsored options can still win when they actually meet
Finn's stated need. No mysterious “ethical score” supplies the answer.

**Midpoint reversal:** After Finn sets his needs, the fictional recommender hides
the height row and reranks the poor-fit item under a larger `BEST VALUE` badge.
Finn's criteria and comparisons remain saved; `$0 CHARGED` stays visible.

**Secured consequence:** Finn compares full useful-life fit against `Works for
this / Lasts / Total cost`, with budget treated as a legitimate constraint. New,
used/borrowed where appropriate, and `Buy nothing yet` remain valid. The ending
requires account-holder confirmation and shows `0 ORDERS / $0 CHARGED` until then.
Packaging and replacement appear as consequences, not blame assigned to a
budget-conscious shopper.

**Character/comedy seed:** Chinmay: “The small tripod takes up less screen. That's
basically desk height.” Techno sits beside it and is visibly taller.

**Do not claim:** Sponsored or inexpensive products are necessarily unsafe,
reviews are all fake, or a retailer intentionally recommends dangerous goods.
Do not invent precise labor or environmental scores.

**Evidence base:** Amazon's seller page supports only that Sponsored Products are
paid advertisements designed to increase visibility and sales:
[Sponsored Products](https://sell.amazon.com/advertising/sponsored-products).
The FTC separately documents choice-steering dark patterns across services; it is
not evidence that Amazon uses the fictional preselection pattern:
[Dark-pattern review](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/07/ftc-icpen-gpen-announce-results-review-use-dark-patterns-affecting-subscription-services-privacy).
EPA shopping guidance recommends considering durability, reuse, repair, need, and
end of life: [Think Green Before You Shop](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/think-green-you-shop-text-only).

**Visual translation seed:** Marketplace navigation, product comparison, and cart
grammar with original marks and icons; sponsored cards slide upward while total
cost and physical waste expand downward.

## 9. Spotty-Fi — The Artist Who Disappeared

**Lesson:** AI use is not the dividing line; creator consent and honest credit are.

**Useful promise:** Finn uses Spotty-Fi to discover artists, share playlists, and
find music that fits a mood. He especially follows a local teen artist whose live
show he and Amy recently attended.

**Corrupted contradiction:** `100% YOU` fills the screen with eerily perfect songs
attributed to slightly different fake artist names. Near-identical tiles multiply
until the original local artist vanishes from view.

**Reading clue:** A fictional case-file statement supplied by the local artist—not
Finn's ear—states that one profile used her voice without permission. A second
artist clearly describes using an AI tool with permission as part of their own
work, preserving the distinction between assistance and impersonation.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** Finn opens credits and the official artist
channel, groups obvious duplicates, and routes the supplied claim for review.
Player-facing states are `Artist says authorized`, `Artist says unauthorized`,
`Platform review pending`, and `Not enough information`. `Save for later` is a
valid choice. Finn does not identify AI or cloning by sound or publicly accuse
anyone.

**Midpoint reversal:** The service merges every creator into one generated
`PERFECT ARTIST` profile and hides credits entirely. Saved credit evidence remains
visible while the recommendation cloud grows opaque.

**Secured consequence:** Finn restores the verified creator profile, follows the
artist, and helps route her supplied claim for platform review. A simple credits
drawer keeps `Undisclosed`, `Artist-reported unauthorized`, and `Platform review
pending` distinct. The final queue reflects Finn's choices without treating
undisclosed work as guilty or asking a minor to adjudicate consent.

**Character/comedy seed:** Chinmay: “The artist credits were cluttering the vibe.”
Techno recognizes the original track because it contains the real squeaky-ball
sound; this is a clue only if the passage establishes it.

**Do not claim:** All AI-assisted music is stolen, fake, unethical, or legally
unprotected; voice similarity proves cloning; a missing disclosure proves human
authorship; or one stream has a simple fixed royalty value.

**Evidence base:** Spotify distinguishes unauthorized voice impersonation from
some authorized or parodic uses:
[Voice impersonation policy](https://support.spotify.com/st-en/artists/article/music-that-impersonates-another-artists-voice/).
The U.S. Copyright Office separately explains that AI assistance does not
automatically eliminate protection for human-authored portions or contributions;
it does not establish voice consent, attribution, or payment:
[AI copyrightability report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf).

**Visual translation seed:** Dark music-library and queue grammar with an original
cool accent, original album art and controls, multiplying near-duplicate tiles,
and a credits drawer rather than a good/bad authenticity meter.

## 10. MapGuess — The Two-Minute Lake Route

**Lesson:** Confidence needs anchors. Confirm the destination, compare the route
with roads and landmarks, and notice sponsored suggestions.

**Useful promise:** MapGuess helps Finn reach an unfamiliar place. While planning
at home, he needs to reach the library study room before Amy's reservation begins.

**Corrupted contradiction:** Finn asks for the library. A confident two-minute
route crosses a lake while a clearly labeled square `Sponsored` snack-shop pin
appears nearby. The game's corrupted AI—not normal ad placement—tries to substitute
that suggestion for Finn's intended destination. No real travel has started.

**Reading clue:** The passage provides the library's street, a named bridge, and
a landmark—the three real-world facts that should not move.

**Player hypothesis and choice:** Finn compares three plausible route cards: the
confident two-minute lake route, a sponsored detour, and a less flashy route that
matches the street, bridge, and landmark. He makes one decisive choice using the
reading clue while remaining stationary.

**Midpoint reversal:** The destination pin moves, but the road and `2 MIN` estimate
do not. The three reading-derived anchors stay fixed, making the contradiction
obvious before Amy names it.

**Secured consequence:** The player locks `DESTINATION / ROAD / LANDMARK` before
moving. The sponsored pin remains clearly labeled as an optional nearby place,
and the final route reaches the library before the reservation. The game never
claims a universally safest route or asks the player to interact while walking,
cycling, or driving.

**Character/comedy seed:** Chinmay: “Good news—we moved where you were going.”
Techno pins her ball beside the real destination only after Finn locks it.

**Do not claim:** Advertisers buy covert route changes, promoted pins are always
deceptive, navigation tools are generally untrustworthy, or players should
interact with controls while driving.

**Evidence base:** Google documents promoted pins and other Maps ads, including
placements along routes, and explains their visible labels:
[Advertising in Maps](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/16388390?hl=en),
[About ads in Maps](https://support.google.com/maps/answer/9947218?hl=en).
Google also documents correction paths for wrong directions and map information:
[Report wrong directions](https://support.google.com/maps/answer/6194894?hl=EN).

**Visual translation seed:** Map, route, pin, and destination-card grammar built
from original map data and symbols; a fixed bridge and lake contrast with the
sliding destination and optional square sponsored marker.

## Common parody and teaching guardrails

- Use no more than the minimum high-level cues needed for recognizable
  transformative commentary: page archetype, transformed color behavior, or one
  familiar information relationship. Combine them with conspicuous original
  naming and overall presentation.
- Create original names, typography, icon families, illustrations, data, copy,
  spacing, proportions, card shapes, and motion.
- Never import screenshots, real posts, logos, product art, thumbnails, album
  covers, map tiles, or exact branded controls.
- Player-facing copy identifies fictional practice sites and uses no real company
  names, real people, or real data. Do not reproduce overall trade dress, pixel
  geometry, signature animation or sound, or a near-identical wordmark.
- A disclaimer supports differentiation but is not legal clearance. Obtain
  qualified intellectual-property review before public release.
- Critique a documented feature or incentive rather than users, political groups,
  or an entire medium.
- Show why the service is useful before exaggerating its failure mode.
- Let the player discover evidence and make the correction; do not reward
  obedience to Amy.
- Use one lesson, one primary joke, and one memorable visual consequence per site.

## Campaign-level open escalation

The plots work whether the local corruption is caused by Chinmay's rushed AI, a
broader autonomous recovery system, or another campaign-wide antagonist. That
causal role should be approved once for the campaign rather than improvised per
site. Exact dialogue remains downstream of plot approval.
